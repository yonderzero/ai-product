# Methods for framing the right problem

Use these as tools, in plain language. Name a method only when it helps the product manager.

## Jobs to be Done

Customers don't buy a product; they hire it to get a job done. (Clayton Christensen, Bob Moesta.)

**The job statement:** *When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].*

- The **struggling moment**: the moment the job gets hard enough that someone looks for help.
- A job has three sides: **functional** (get it done), **emotional** (how they want to feel), **social** (how they want to be seen).
- Ask "why?" until you reach the job behind the task. "Fill in the visit note" → "prove the visit happened and the care was right" → "keep the agency's licence and the family's trust".

### The job map

Every job runs through the same steps (Tony Ulwick, Strategyn). Map the customer's job onto them and mark which steps the product covers today:

| Step | What the customer does |
|---|---|
| Define | Decide what needs to be done and plan it |
| Locate | Gather the inputs and information needed |
| Prepare | Set up the work and the environment |
| Confirm | Check everything is ready before starting |
| Execute | Do the job |
| Monitor | Check it's going to plan |
| Modify | Fix and adjust when it isn't |
| Conclude | Finish, record, hand over, learn |

A level 1 product usually covers execute and conclude. A level 4 product owns the job from locate to conclude. The steps the product doesn't cover are where the bigger framing usually is.

### The four forces

Whether customers switch to something new (Bob Moesta, *Demand-Side Sales*):

| Force | Direction | The question |
|---|---|---|
| **Push** | towards change | What makes today's way painful enough to change? |
| **Pull** | towards change | What makes the new way attractive? |
| **Anxiety** | against change | What worries them about the new way? (With AI: trust, errors, losing control, jobs.) |
| **Habit** | against change | What keeps them doing it the old way? |

A problem is worth solving when push and pull clearly beat anxiety and habit, or when the product can lower the anxiety and break the habit.

## The Mom Test

Customers will lie to be nice. Judge evidence by what people did, not what they say they'd do. (Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*.)

**Strong evidence:**
- a specific past event in their own words ("last Tuesday, two carers called in sick and…");
- time or money they already spend on a workaround;
- what they tried before, and why it failed;
- a commitment: time, a pilot, a referral, a budget.

**Weak evidence (grade it Assumption):**
- compliments ("that's a great idea");
- opinions about the future ("I'd definitely use that");
- hypotheticals ("we would pay for…");
- the company describing its own customers' pain.

**Good questions** for the riskiest assumptions are about their life, the past and specifics:
- "Tell me about the last time that happened."
- "What did you do about it? What did that cost?"
- "What have you tried? Why didn't it work?"
- "Who else is involved? Who decides?"

Never: "Would you use…?", "Would you pay…?", "Do you think it's a good idea?"

## BHAG

A Big Hairy Audacious Goal (Jim Collins and Jerry Porras, *Built to Last*). For the problem, it's what would be true if the problem were solved completely, for every customer.

A good BHAG is:
- **clear and measurable**: you could tell if it happened;
- **dated**: a few years out, typically around 2029–2030;
- **bold**: it feels slightly uncomfortable, maybe a 50–70% chance;
- **about the customer's job**, not the company's revenue.

"Transform care with AI" isn't one. "No carer writes a visit note by hand by 2029" is.

## Evidence grades

The same grades as the opportunities, with the Mom Test applied:

- **Strong:** three or more findings, from two or more kinds of source, including past behaviour or spend from someone other than the company itself.
- **Good:** two or more findings, or one specific past event.
- **Thin:** one finding.
- **Assumption:** opinions, hypotheticals, compliments, or the product manager's belief only.
